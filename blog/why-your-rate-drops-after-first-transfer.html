Why Your Money Transfer Rate Gets Worse After the First Send

Sep 2026 · 5 min read

If your first transfer to the Philippines got a great rate and every one since has felt a little worse, you're not imagining it — and it's not the market moving against you.

Same day, same $500, two different rates

Open two money transfer apps on the same day, send the same $500, and you can get two very different numbers. One quotes a rate of 61.9. Another quotes 58.2. That's nearly a 4-peso spread on a single dollar — which on $500 works out to roughly ₱2,000, just depending on which app you happened to open first.

The usual explanation is "exchange rates move." Sometimes that's true. But often the real answer is simpler and less flattering to the app: the good rate was never the real rate. It was a first-transfer promo, and it was never going to stick around.

The bait: how the promo rate works

Most money transfer companies advertise their best-looking number to get you to sign up — a one-time rate, usually only good for your first transfer, sometimes only up to a certain amount. It's the number in the ad, the number in the app store screenshot, the number that shows up when you search "best rate to send money to the Philippines."

Once you're a customer, that rate quietly disappears. Your second transfer, your tenth, your fiftieth — they all go through at the standard rate, which is worse, sometimes by a meaningful amount. Nobody sends a push notification announcing the downgrade. You just notice, eventually, that your family seems to be getting a little less each time, and assume the market shifted.

Why they do it

It's not a trick unique to any one company — it's closer to the default playbook for the industry. Acquiring a new customer costs money, so companies front-load their best offer to win the sign-up, then make the economics work on the transfers that follow. For a company, a first-transfer promo is a customer-acquisition cost. For you, it's the only rate you'll ever be shown before you've already committed.

That's the part that matters most for repeat senders — and if you're supporting family back home, you're almost always a repeat sender. The rate that's actually relevant to you isn't the one in the ad. It's the one you'll be getting on transfer number six.

How to tell if you're looking at a promo or a standard rate

  • Check if it's labeled "new customer" or "first transfer." If a rate has either phrase anywhere near it, assume it won't repeat.
  • Compare it to the mid-market rate. This is the true, un-marked-up rate you'd get on something like a currency converter site — no company can legitimately beat it on a standard transfer. If an advertised rate is above the mid-market rate, it's a promo, full stop.
  • Log in and check your account, not the homepage. The rate shown to an existing, logged-in user for a repeat transfer is usually the real one. The rate shown to a visitor on the marketing page usually isn't.
  • Be suspicious of rates that look too good on your fifth transfer. If a "good deal" shows up again out of nowhere, check whether it's tied to a new promotion, not just a normal rate.
